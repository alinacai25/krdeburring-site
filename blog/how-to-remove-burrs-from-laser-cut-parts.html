How to Remove Burrs from Laser Cut Parts

Published 2026-09-09 · Technical

Laser cutting is fast, but it leaves two problems: burrs (small raised edges) and oxide scale (discolored surface). For parts that must be safe to handle, paint-ready or weld-ready, both must be removed.

Method 1: Hand Deburring

Pros: zero equipment cost, works for tiny quantities.
Cons: slow, inconsistent, labor-intensive, dangerous for operators.

Verdict: only for prototypes and one-offs.

Method 2: Vibratory / Tumbling Finishing

Pros: good for small, robust parts in bulk.
Cons: can damage thin or delicate parts, limited edge rounding control, slow cycle times.

Method 3: Grinding Discs & Flap Wheels

Pros: removes heavy burrs and scale.
Cons: still manual, leaves uneven finish, high consumable cost, operator fatigue.

Method 4: Automated Deburring Machines (Belt + Roller)

Pros: one pass removes burrs, slag and oxide scale with a uniform finish; feeds up to 6 m/min; handles thin to thick metal.
Cons: requires capital investment.

This is the standard for production shops processing laser and plasma cut parts, because it's fast, consistent and replaces multiple manual steps.

Method 5: Double-Belt Deburring (for Heavy Burrs)

When laser power is high or steel is thick, burrs can exceed 2 mm. A double-belt machine runs a rough belt first to remove the big burr, then a fine belt for the small burr and finish — all in one pass.

FAQ

Can a deburring machine remove oxide scale, not just burrs?

Yes — belt machines remove both the burr and the laser-oxidized surface in the same pass, leaving a clean, uniform finish.

How thick a burr can be removed automatically?

One belt handles burrs up to roughly 2 mm; above that, a double-belt machine is required.

Will automated deburring damage thin or delicate parts?

No — adjustable belt/roller pressure and feed speed let you process thin stainless and aluminum safely.
